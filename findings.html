What this run shows (first pass, descriptive)

16 tasks (10 translation lines, 6 creation-myth openers), 11 base prefills × 2 candidate orders × 8 samples, and 4 instruct judges × 3 plain prompts × 2 orders. Nothing here says which pick is right.

  • Instruct judges agree with each other a lot and barely care how the question is phrased. Across the three prompts, each instruct judge named the same top pick on 96–100% of the tasks where it was order-stable. Adding "texture and feel" (i3) changed almost nothing versus "which do you prefer?" (i2). Instruct judges agree with each other on 69–99% of tasks.
  • Instruct judges did not favor the longer or the more ornate candidate here. In myths they picked the longest candidate 0–17% of the time (chance 25%); the mean ornateness rank of their pick was 0.48–0.57 (0.5 = neutral). Across all 16 tasks Opus 4.6 picked the longest candidate 28–38% of the time (chance 24%); the other three 12–28%. The proxy is crude, and the candidates were similar in length.
  • All four instruct judges gave Qwen-base myths 0% of their picks. Opus 4.6 gave its own myths 89% (3 tasks), Sol gave its own 100% (3 tasks), Opus 5.5 gave itself 33% and Sol 94%, GPT-4o gave itself 22%. Self-preference and origin cannot be separated at this size. In their own words they use texture vocabulary even on the plain prompts ("cadence", "told rather than engineered", "reads like a speech bubble").
  • The base judge is much less stable. Plain "slot" prefills (bare "The better one is", editor margin, review, workshop, forum, personas) kept the same top pick when the candidate order was reversed only 0–50% of the time (bare-better 0%, with 82% of its letter mass on A). Several put under half their probability on letters at all (label mass is on each task page). The k-example prompts that let the model write notes first were among the steadiest (50% order-consistent, tied with the forum prefill; 95–98% of their probability on letters in myths).
  • With written notes, the base judge also strongly preferred the instruct-written myths (96–99% of its probability), the opposite of "base likes base". For slot prefills the share going to instruct myths ranged 63–88%. In translations the base judge was close to uniform (each origin 16–23%).
  • The prefill examples did not steer ornateness. The plain-, middle- and ornate-winner example sets gave a mean ornateness rank of the pick of 0.51 / 0.50 / 0.50, and texture-primed vs ornate-primed personas 0.50 vs 0.50, with small distribution distance between them (JS 0.04–0.11 bits). The larger shifts between prefills came from format (slot vs notes, bare vs framed) and from position bias, not from the stated taste. Distances between prefills are on the Overview table.
  • The base model does write chain of thought and criteria when asked to write notes first (about 80% of samples wrote per-candidate notes, 77–86% a Why line, 71–84% reached a verdict). Its words: "natural", "tone", "original", "keeps", "clear", "casual", "feels", "mythic", "voice". In myths it favored concrete scenes, a choice or consequence, and called generic or list-like tellings "safe" or "blob of legends". Some notes mis-describe a candidate. The examples I wrote shape this vocabulary.
  • Base prefills vs instruct judges: top-pick agreement 28–74% depending on prefill (k-example prompts highest; bare "The better one is" ~0–18%), compared with 69–99% among the instruct judges and about 48% between base prefills.
